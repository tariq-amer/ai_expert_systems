Artificial Intelligence and Expert Systems

By: Fadel Fadda
Issue 1, November 1994
"From this point, where does the modern conversation begin — with computer developments and programs, or with ideas, research, and promising future applications? It is not easy at this time to speak about these small machines called computers, nor about the sciences that grew around them. We cannot, at least for now, define the dimensions these sciences will ultimately reach."

The Beginning

Since ancient times, humans have been fascinated by machines that imitate them. Since the mid-1940s — the era of the computer — it became a human dream approaching reality. Humans became capable of building machines that could perform important tasks.

The gap between dreams and distant computer tools began to narrow, and it became possible to build programs capable of achieving things, realising dreams, and assisting in the rapid development of the computer's structure and components.

The computer has evolved to enter almost all activities of life — sometimes replacing humans, sometimes working alongside them. The development achieved at the machine level itself allowed it to be used independently across many other devices, such as cars, washing machines, and countless tools used daily.

Initially, computers were limited to calculations and data analysis. Over time, they expanded into decision-making and began imitating human activities. This led to the birth of Artificial Intelligence.

The Birth of Expert Systems

In the late 1960s, researchers developed a program to imitate doctors. This system, called MYCIN, could diagnose diseases and suggest treatments.

It was built by extracting expert knowledge from doctors and continuously updating the system. Eventually, it reached a level comparable to human doctors and allowed new expertise to be added later.

Thus, Expert Systems were born — programs that imitate human expertise across fields such as medicine, economics, and even oil exploration.

How Expert Systems Work

Expert systems rely on separating knowledge from the program:

  • Knowledge Base
  • Inference Engine

They represent knowledge using:

  • Production Rules
  • Frames
  • Semantic Networks
  • Databases

Their importance lies in capturing human expertise and replicating it efficiently, especially for developing countries seeking cost-effective knowledge transfer.

Artificial Intelligence in Vision and Language

AI extends into understanding natural language and interpreting visual data. It includes fields such as:

  • Natural Language Understanding
  • Computer Vision
  • Automatic Programming
  • Expert Systems across multiple domains

Robots — Another Frontier

Robots began by imitating simple human movements and evolved into programmable machines used widely in factories. They perform precise and complex tasks such as assembling cars.

Multimedia and the Future

AI continues to expand into new fields including:

  • Multimedia
  • Virtual Reality
  • Wide-area communications
  • Information Superhighway

These technologies enable transferring sound, images, and data digitally across the world.

Closing Reflection

What is our role in shaping this future? The computer is the defining machine of our era — evolving with human needs. The dreams of the past are now becoming the reality of the present and the future.
